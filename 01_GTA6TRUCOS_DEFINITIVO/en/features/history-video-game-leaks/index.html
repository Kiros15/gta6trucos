The History of Video Game Leaks: From Half-Life 2 to GTA VI

13 October 2026

From stolen prototypes to pre-release story spoilers, we explore how leaks reshaped the video game industry and what it all means for GTA VI.

This report distinguishes confirmed facts, leaks and commentary. We do not reproduce stolen materials or personal data.

Secrets before social media

Long before social media, magazines, retailers and distributors occasionally revealed games ahead of schedule. An early product listing or preview build could spoil a reveal. Today, the difference is speed: information spreads within seconds. Crucially, an early announcement is not the same as stolen development footage or a breach exposing personal information.

Half-Life 2: the 2003 source-code theft

In October 2003, Valve confirmed that portions of Half-Life 2 source code had been stolen and circulated. Gabe Newell asked the community for help investigating. The case illustrated that leaks could compromise technical assets, not just marketing plans. Half-Life 2 eventually launched in November 2004. Its delays cannot responsibly be attributed to the theft alone.

E3 and the reveals that lost their surprise

At the height of E3, publishers orchestrated major announcements months in advance. A retailer listing, a photograph or an accidentally published product page could spoil the reveal. Publishers lost control of the timing; some players enjoyed the early discovery. Extra conversation, however, is not proof of increased sales.

The Last of Us Part II: spoilers before release

In April 2020, videos and major story details from The Last of Us Part II circulated before launch. Naughty Dog asked fans not to spread spoilers. Scenes viewed out of context could also lead to misleading interpretations. The delay announced earlier that month was attributed to pandemic-related logistics, not established as a consequence of the leak.

GTA VI: the September 2022 leak

On September 18, 2022, dozens of videos from an early Grand Theft Auto VI build surfaced online. They showed debugging tools, unfinished environments, driving and characters. Rockstar confirmed a network intrusion and unauthorized access to development material. Some viewers mistook an unfinished prototype for the intended final quality. Rockstar said work would continue; the leak itself is not evidence that later schedule changes were caused by it.

Insomniac Games: the human cost of a breach

In December 2023, an attack on Insomniac Games led to internal documents and development material being published, including information related to Marvel’s Wolverine. More seriously, personal data relating to employees and collaborators was exposed. The incident demonstrates how a leak can become a genuine privacy and safety issue rather than mere entertainment news.

Why leaks happen and who they affect

Causes range from cyberattacks and insider disclosures to retail mistakes and accidental publication. Effects extend across marketing, security, development, employees and players. Investigations cost money; spoilers damage surprises; unfinished footage can fuel misinformation and unrealistic expectations. Claims that a leak was deliberately staged for publicity require evidence.

AI makes verification harder

Generative tools can produce convincing mock screenshots, developer interfaces and character footage. A purported leaked image is therefore not sufficient proof. Publishers should trace provenance, corroborate details and distinguish verified facts from speculation. Sharing stolen personal data should never be justified by the pursuit of traffic.

Conclusion: do we really want to know everything before playing?

Curiosity is part of gaming culture, but not every piece of circulating information deserves amplification. From Half-Life 2 to GTA VI, the history of leaks shows that excitement and harm can coexist. The more we anticipate a game, the more we want to know—and the more we risk losing the very surprise that made it special.
